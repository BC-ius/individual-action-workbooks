Opening · Full source text v5.4

Start here

Feedback is part of how we work with other people. It helps us understand what is working well, what could be improved and how our actions affect the work and people around us. Sometimes we give feedback to a colleague, sometimes we receive it, and sometimes we need to ask for another person’s perspective.

But not all feedback is useful. It can be too vague, based on assumptions, poorly timed or difficult to act on. The challenge is recognising when feedback has a worthwhile purpose, exchanging information clearly and constructively, and deciding what to do with it.

This guide helps you practise two connected capabilities: giving useful feedback when it is worth giving, and receiving feedback well enough to decide what it means. Sometimes feedback arrives without you asking for it. Sometimes you deliberately seek another person’s perspective because they can see something you cannot.

The aim

The aim is not more feedback. It is better judgement about when feedback is useful, giving feedback that is clear, specific and purposeful, receiving feedback in a way that helps you understand what is being communicated, and responding appropriately to what you learn.

What this guide will help you practise

  • Recognising when feedback could genuinely be useful - and when it may not be.
  • Making information clear enough to be useful when you give feedback.
  • Receiving feedback without confusing listening with automatic agreement.
  • Deliberately seeking a useful perspective when another person can see something you need to understand.
  • Learning from real feedback situations rather than only learning a technique.

How to use this guide

This is not designed to be completed in one sitting. Work through the early sections in short sittings if that suits your work. They help you understand the capability and prepare for real application. Most development then happens in normal work: using feedback selectively, noticing what happens and returning later to identify what is changing.

The structures and questions in this guide are guides for your thinking while you build experience. They are not rules that every feedback situation has to follow.

About the journey and time commitment

This is a spaced workplace-development journey, not a short course. The deliberate guide time is relatively limited, but the elapsed journey depends on when genuine feedback situations arise. Some people may have enough useful experience to move into consolidation after a couple of weeks; others may need several weeks or longer. Do not create feedback opportunities simply to finish the guide.

The estimates above are deliberate guide time, not elapsed calendar time. They assume that you think through the prompts and record the short conclusions that matter. If you choose to use the optional notes areas in depth, allow longer. The return reminder later in the guide is a check-in point, not a completion deadline.

Thinking, writing and support

You do not need to write down every thought or fill every notes area. Think through the prompts, then record the short conclusions worth returning to. A few words may be enough. The time estimates allow for thinking and review; they are not required sitting lengths.

Do not create feedback opportunities simply to complete an exercise. Use real situations where feedback could genuinely help.

You can complete the guide privately and independently. If a manager, colleague, peer, mentor or another trusted person can usefully support your development, involve them where that adds value. They are a sounding board, not an assessor.

The Feedback Job Aid is at the end. It is signposted again when you start applying the capability so you can find it quickly. In a digital copy, use this shortcut: Feedback Job Aid

Scope of this guide

This guide is about ordinary workplace feedback between colleagues. Formal performance, conduct, safety, harassment, discrimination, grievance or other serious matters may require a manager, HR, safety or another organisational process rather than direct peer feedback.

Stage 1 · Full source text v5.4

Why feedback matters

Start by understanding what useful feedback can give you - and why knowing about feedback is easier than using it well.

Some information is difficult to get on your own

You can see some of the results of your own work for yourself. But other people often have information that you cannot easily generate alone. A colleague may experience your handover, use your work, work alongside you during a busy period, hear how you explained something, or see an effect that is less obvious from your position.

Their perspective can help you adjust something, repeat something that works, investigate a problem earlier or understand how your work and behaviour affect other people. In the same way, you may hold useful information about how somebody else’s work or behaviour affects you and the work you share.

Feedback is not only about correcting mistakes

Everyday workplace feedback can concern the work itself, the way people work together, or something worth reinforcing because it helped.

Work or performance feedback might concern an output, a handover, a decision, clarity, timing, coordination or what made a task easier or harder to complete.

Behaviour feedback might concern how someone communicates, collaborates, interrupts, responds under pressure or affects people working around them.

Reinforcing feedback identifies something that worked and why it helped, making the useful information more specific than a general “thanks” or “good job”.

These often overlap. A behaviour matters because it affects the work, the relationship or the working environment.

Knowing about feedback is easier than using it

The basic idea is familiar to most people. Real situations are harder. You may know that something is worth saying but decide it is easier not to. You may soften a point so much that the other person does not understand it. Someone may give you feedback and your first reaction is to explain why they are wrong. Or you may want another perspective and ask a broad question that produces little more than “Looks fine”.

That gap between knowing feedback can be useful and using it effectively is what this guide is designed to help with.

Receiving feedback is not the same as accepting it

When receiving feedback, your first reaction does not necessarily tell you whether it has value. You may disagree, feel defensive or simply not know what to do with it. First, make sure you understand what is being communicated. This is particularly important when feedback relates to your responsibilities or expectations at work: you may need to act on it, even if you first need clarification or a better understanding of the circumstances.

Let’s be real: feedback may arrive when you did not ask for it

A colleague or manager may comment in the middle of ordinary work. You may have no preparation time and may not like the way the point is delivered. Your first job is simply to understand what information is actually being offered before deciding what, if anything, to do with it.

Stage 2 · Workplace context

Where does feedback show up in your work?

Look at how feedback works among the people you work with. Notice what helps, what gets in the way and what could be better.

Feedback is part of everyday work, not just planned conversations. It may concern the work itself, how people behave towards each other or something worth recognising and repeating.

Giving feedback

You notice something about a colleague’s work or behaviour that you think is worth addressing. Perhaps it caused a problem, could be improved, needs clarification or deserves recognition because it worked well. You may be unsure whether to raise it, how to explain your concern or how the other person will respond.

Receiving feedback

Someone comments on your work or behaviour, or you deliberately ask for another perspective. You may find the feedback helpful, unclear, unexpected or difficult to hear. The challenge may be understanding what is meant, managing your initial reaction, making sense of the information or responding appropriately.

How well does feedback work in your workplace?

Think about the colleagues and situations you know. You do not need to assess the whole organisation, or know what happens in conversations you have not observed.

Where does feedback show up? Is it used openly and constructively, avoided, or a mixture of both? Think about feedback that is given, received and actively sought.

When does it work well? What makes it useful, and what difference does it make to the work or to people working together?

When does it work less well? What seems to get in the way — for example, unclear messages, working habits, time pressure, limited trust, different levels of authority or concerns about consequences?

If people used feedback more effectively, what might work better in the workplace?

Think these questions through. You do not need to record a response to each one.

Stage 3 · Personal focus and value

Where could you make better use of feedback?

Look at your own experience of giving, receiving or seeking feedback. Identify what you could handle better and why it would matter.

Think of a recent situation involving feedback at work. It might be a time when you gave feedback about someone’s work or behaviour; received feedback, whether expected or not; asked someone for another perspective; or thought about giving feedback but did not. You might also have realised afterwards that feedback could have helped. Choose an ordinary situation that tells you something about how you currently handle feedback.

How do you currently handle feedback?

Looking back at that situation, consider: What happened, and what did you do — or choose not to do? What influenced your decision or actions? How well did you handle the situation — was there anything you avoided, missed or found difficult? What happened as a result, as far as you know? These are prompts for thinking, not separate written responses.

What could you handle more effectively?

Looking back, is there something you might have approached differently, an opportunity you missed or something you already do well but would like to use more consistently? Consider the circumstances as well as your own actions.

What difference would improving make?

If you handled similar feedback situations more effectively, what could change for you, for the people you work with or for the work itself? Choose a difference that matters to you.

You do not have to write out the whole example. Keep two short conclusions to return to as you practise.

Understanding feedback

Why feedback provides useful information, and why separating observation, impact and assumptions matters.

How feedback works

Imagine you prepare a handover for a colleague. You believe everything is clear, but they cannot tell which actions are still outstanding. You know what you intended to communicate; your colleague knows what it was like to receive and use the information.

If they explain what was unclear and how it affected their work, you gain information that you might not have discovered on your own. You may also have information that helps them understand the situation. Feedback helps close these gaps in understanding.

Feedback provides information about work or behaviour. Its usefulness depends on the quality of that information, how well it is understood and what someone can do with it.

Feedback functionWhat it means
Judge the opportunityIs exchanging feedback worthwhile and appropriate?
Exchange usable informationGive or seek specific, relevant information.
Make sense of itUnderstand and evaluate what you learn.
Decide what followsContinue, adjust, investigate or take another appropriate response.

These are connected functions, not compulsory steps in a conversation. You may initiate feedback, seek it deliberately or receive it unexpectedly.

Why feedback helps or hinders

Compare two comments about a presentation:

General judgement
“You're not very good at explaining things.”
Specific information
“When you presented the figures this morning, you moved straight into the detailed calculations. I wasn't sure what decision you wanted us to make.”

The first directs attention towards a judgement about the person. They may start defending their competence without knowing what to change.

The second identifies something they can consider: would explaining the purpose before presenting the figures have helped?

Even specific feedback can be incomplete or wrong, and useful information can be uncomfortable to hear. The aim is to understand the work or behaviour, not to deliver or accept a verdict about the person.

Feedback can help someone continue something effective (reinforcing feedback) or understand, reconsider or improve something (developmental feedback). “Thanks, that helped” expresses appreciation; explaining what helped and why provides reinforcing information.

Start with what you know — not the story you have already built

During a busy period, a colleague leaves a shared work area twice without telling you. You handle the incoming work alone, become frustrated and start thinking they are deliberately leaving everything to you. Separate three things:

ElementIn this situation
Observation — what you directly saw, heard or can establish from work evidence such as an output, record or result.Your colleague left the work area twice without telling you.
Impact — the consequence or personally experienced effect.You handled the incoming work alone and found it difficult to keep up.
Assumptions — what you think happened, what it means or why someone acted that way.You assume your colleague deliberately left their share of the work to you.

Before giving feedback, ask yourself whether the concern is based on what happened or on an assumption. Your colleague may have been asked to help elsewhere. If your concern depends on an assumption, check it first.

If their behaviour affected your work, you can raise that directly without knowing why they did it. Describe what they did and the effect it had, rather than attributing motives.

When receiving feedback, avoid assuming why someone is giving it. Clarify what they observed and how it affected them; their explanation may also contain assumptions about you.

Think of a time when a colleague frustrated you. What did you know, and what were you assuming?

Stage 4 · Page 2 of 4

Giving useful feedback

Decide when feedback is worth giving, use SBI to make the point clear and consider an appropriate way forward.

Is it worth giving feedback?

A colleague prepares a handover differently from the way you would. If you can find everything you need and continue without difficulty, the difference may simply be a preference. If the handover does not identify outstanding actions and causes rework, there is a clearer reason to raise it.

Before giving feedback, consider three things:

Would it help? What could the person understand, continue or do differently? Is there a meaningful benefit, or are you mainly expressing irritation?

Do you know enough? Can you describe what happened and why it matters? Check relevant facts first if you are relying on assumptions or second-hand information.

Is this the right time and approach? Are you an appropriate person to raise it? Consider privacy, time pressure, the relationship and whether direct feedback would be constructive and reasonably safe.

Sometimes a question, a straightforward request or another route is more useful. Deciding not to give feedback can be good judgement; avoiding every worthwhile but uncomfortable conversation is different. If now is not a suitable moment, you can return to the issue later.

Constructing a useful message

A useful structure is Situation–Behaviour–Impact (SBI). It helps you explain your point clearly. Where appropriate, explore the other person’s perspective or suggest a way forward.

Return to the incomplete handover. You spent time rechecking completed work and identified an outstanding action late. Your first reaction might be that your colleague needs to be more careful. That judgement does not tell them what needs to be different.

Bring these together:

“When you prepared yesterday’s customer handover, you didn’t distinguish between completed and outstanding actions. I spent time rechecking completed work and picked up one action late. Next time, could you clearly mark which actions are still outstanding?”

Be direct about what the person did without judging their character. If a useful alternative is clear, recommend it or make a reasonable request. If you are unsure what would help, ask for their perspective first. You can combine inquiry and a suggestion, and adapt your proposal as you learn more.

For a more substantial conversation, a brief opening can help: “Could we have a quick chat about yesterday's handover? There's something I think could make the next one easier.” Choose a suitable moment and make the point clear without softening it until its meaning disappears. The same structure works for reinforcing feedback:

“In this morning's handover, you flagged the outstanding actions first. That meant I knew exactly where to pick up.”

The colleague knows what worked and why. That brief comment may be enough; you could also encourage them to keep doing it. Developmental feedback may need more discussion before deciding what would help.

Make it an exchange

A colleague changes figures shortly before a shared deadline, creating rework for you.

You: “When you changed the figures just before yesterday's deadline, I had to redo the summary. What happened from your side?”

Colleague: “The customer only confirmed the final figures that morning. The earlier ones would have been wrong.”

The disruption was real, but the colleague may not have been able to make the change earlier. Listen to the explanation rather than defend your first interpretation.

You: “I see. Was there a way we could have known earlier that the figures might change? That would have helped me plan the summary differently.”

The next step might be to agree how to flag possible changes rather than demand earlier final figures.

What if the colleague disagrees instead? “I already warned you those figures might change.”

You: “I don't remember seeing that. Can we check when it was communicated? I'd like to work out how we can avoid the rework next time.”

You may have missed the warning, or understood it differently. Explore the difference before deciding. You can acknowledge what you learn while retaining a legitimate concern. If a change is worthwhile, discuss something specific that each of you can reasonably do. You do not need agreement on every interpretation, and if the person declines or the exchange becomes unproductive, do not force it.

When a colleague's behaviour affects you

Imagine a colleague frequently takes calls on speakerphone in your shared workspace. You find it difficult to concentrate. Calling them inconsiderate expresses a judgement but does little to establish a workable arrangement. Raise the specific behaviour and your own experience, privately if appropriate:

“This morning, you had two calls on speaker while I was working on the customer figures. I found it difficult to concentrate. Could we work out somewhere more suitable for speaker calls?”

They may not have realised the impact or may need to use the space for a practical reason. Ask about their circumstances and explore what could work for both of you. You do not have to prove that everyone is disturbed or that the behaviour was intentional.

You are making a reasonable request about shared working conditions, not assuming authority to manage your colleague. Sometimes the request alone is enough. If the issue cannot be resolved, a manager or person responsible for the workspace may need to help.

Where behaviour is harmful, threatening, discriminatory or repeatedly humiliating, or direct discussion would expose you to unreasonable risk, use appropriate organisational support. You do not need to establish a formal label or confront the person before seeking help.

Receiving and seeking feedback

Understand, clarify and evaluate feedback. Seek another person's perspective deliberately when it would help.

Receiving well involves managing your first reaction, understanding what someone means, evaluating the information, deciding what it warrants and responding appropriately. You do not need to do all of these things during the conversation. Understanding feedback does not mean agreeing with it.

UnderstandHear the information before judging it.
ClarifyFind out what was actually observed or meant.
EvaluateConsider evidence, perspective and relevance.
Respond appropriatelyDecide what, if anything, the information warrants.

These are useful areas to consider, not a required four-step conversation. The final area is explained in the fourth part of Stage 4.

Hear the feedback before judging it

A colleague says after a meeting:

“You dominate these meetings. Nobody else gets a chance to speak.”

You feel defensive. You have been trying to keep the project moving and want to explain why you needed to take the lead. If you start defending yourself now, you may never discover what prompted the comment.

Give yourself a moment, hear them out and focus first on understanding. “I'm surprised to hear that. Can you tell me a bit more?” If you are too upset to have a useful conversation, ask to return to it later. Feeling defensive does not establish that the feedback is wrong; accepting it calmly does not establish that it is right.

Clarify what the person means

“Dominating meetings” is a judgement. Use the situation, behaviour and impact distinctions from the giving approach to identify missing information.

You: “Can you give me an example of when I've done that?”

Colleague: “Yesterday's project review, for a start. And the planning meeting last week.”

You have the situations, but not the behaviour.

You: “What was I doing in yesterday's review that gave you that impression?”

Colleague: “I was explaining why the delivery date might slip and you started talking before I'd finished.”

You: “What was the point you didn't get to make?”

Colleague: “That we were still waiting for confirmation from the supplier. I don't think everyone understood that.”

Your colleague cannot recall a specific example from the planning meeting. You now understand their main concern: they experienced being interrupted and believe important information was missed. The wider pattern remains unclear. Each question filled a gap. Depending on the feedback, ask for a particular example, the behaviour observed, the expected standard or the effect. Check your understanding if necessary: “So you're concerned that I cut off information we needed?” You need enough detail to understand, not an interrogation.

Evaluate the information

Now consider what the feedback actually establishes.

Information receivedWhat you can reasonably conclude
You spoke before your colleague finished in yesterday's review.You remember doing this. It is a specific observation you recognise.
Your colleague did not get to explain the supplier issue.They are well placed to describe their experience and what they wanted to communicate.
They believe other people did not understand the issue.Possible, but you do not yet know what the other attendees understood.
You dominate meetings.A wider interpretation. One recognised example and another unclear example do not establish a general pattern.

Consider whether the person has relevant knowledge or experience. Your colleague can describe their own experience, but may not know how everyone else experienced the meeting.

The meeting was running late, which helps explain why you interrupted. But it may still have prevented your colleague from making a useful point.

You do not need to investigate every comment. If the issue matters and you remain unsure, ask another colleague what they have noticed.

You can take the interruption seriously without accepting the wider judgement that you dominate meetings. Consider giving people more room to contribute and notice whether it helps. You might finish by saying, “I understand what you're getting at now. I'll think about it.” This acknowledges the information without committing you to change. You can decide later what it warrants. Think about your own experience. Can you recall feedback that you initially disagreed with but later found contained something useful? What helped you see it differently?

Seeking feedback deliberately

Sometimes you need information that will not arrive unless you ask. You may want to understand whether a new approach is helping, why something is difficult or what you should keep doing. Start with the information needed, not a request for approval.

“Any feedback?” leaves the other person to search for something to say. They may offer reassurance or whatever is easiest to remember. Connect three things:

What you need to understandWho could help?What to ask
Whether you leave room for contributions.Someone who attended the relevant meetings.What did you notice about people's opportunities to finish their points when I was leading the discussion?
Whether your handover was easy to use.The colleague who picked up the work.What made the handover easy or difficult to use?

Choose someone with relevant knowledge, observation or experience who can reasonably provide a candid answer. Explain what you want to understand so it is easier for them to offer useful detail.

If they reply, “It was fine,” ask a balanced follow-up: “What, if anything, helped or hindered you? Can you give me an example?” There may be no difficulty to report. If they identify one, hear it out rather than immediately defending your work. Use the same clarification and evaluation approach as when feedback arrives unexpectedly.

Seeking an observation in advance

You can also ask a willing colleague to notice a particular aspect of an upcoming task. This can help them collect more specific first-hand information.

If you want to use this technique, before making the request. It explains how to agree a manageable focus, explore the observations afterwards and recognise the limitations of the information.

Stage 4 · Page 4 of 4

Putting feedback to use

Consider what feedback warrants, adapt to the situation and carry useful learning into your next real application.

Decide what the information warrants

Once you have understood and evaluated feedback, decide what, if anything, it gives you reason to do. Different parts of the information may warrant different responses.

ResponseWhen it makes sense
ReinforceSomething effective is worth continuing.
AdjustThere is a clear reason for a feasible change.
TestA possible change looks promising, but its value remains uncertain.
ClarifyMeaning or expectations remain unclear.
Investigate furtherMore evidence or another perspective would help.
Take no actionThe information does not warrant further effort or change.
Use another routeDifferent authority, support or an organisational process is needed.

Return to the project meetings. You have a recognised example of interrupting someone and a question about whether this happens more generally. You do not need to stop contributing or accept the label “dominating”. Instead, you decide to test giving people more time to respond before offering your own answer.

At the next review you pause after asking about delivery risks. A colleague raises a dependency you had not considered. Another says they had more room to contribute, and the meeting finishes on time.

These are encouraging signs, not proof that the adjustment always works or caused every improvement. Different agendas or people may affect the result. Continue testing in comparable situations where useful.

Deciding on an adjustment and establishing whether it helps are different things. A useful test involves a specific change, a real opportunity to try it and evidence of what happened.

Adapt to the situation

A clear, low-consequence issue may require a brief comment. Ambiguous evidence, different communication expectations, a strained relationship, status differences or significant consequences may require more care, inquiry or another route. Reinforcing feedback is not automatically easy, and developmental feedback is not automatically difficult. Feedback cannot resolve everything. If colleagues have never agreed what a handover should contain, establishing a shared requirement with the process owner may help more than repeated comments. A legitimate work instruction or safety requirement is not simply optional feedback. An immediate operational or safety issue requires the appropriate immediate response. Use the boundaries explained earlier rather than relying on a better conversation to resolve serious concerns.

Recognise useful feedback

In the late-change example, a colleague may disagree with your original explanation but reveal a better way to communicate likely changes. Polite agreement without clearer information may achieve less.

Distinguish what you did, what information became available, how the other person responded, what was decided and what happened in the work afterwards. A well-handled exchange may not produce cooperation; an agreeable conversation may not produce useful learning. Stage 6 provides a fuller guide for making these judgements after your first experience.

From understanding to application

Next comes a period of practice in everyday work. Start by noticing easy, worthwhile opportunities to give specific feedback—for example, recognising something a colleague did well and explaining why it helped. Repeat in genuine situations, learn briefly from each attempt, and increase the challenge only when useful. Remain alert to feedback you receive, but you do not need to wait for it.

Stage 5 · Begin practising

Start with everyday opportunities

For the next few weeks, look for ordinary moments when a short, useful piece of feedback could help. Start with manageable situations rather than difficult conversations.

Your main practice: giving useful feedback

Notice something a colleague does that is genuinely helpful or worth continuing. Instead of a general “good job”, say what they did and why it helped. This is a straightforward way to begin using Situation–Behaviour–Impact (SBI).

When there's a clear, low-consequence improvement to raise, you can practise that too. You don't need to choose a sensitive issue or manufacture feedback just to complete the guide.

Three everyday opportunities to notice

Something worth reinforcing

A colleague flags outstanding actions clearly in a handover. Tell them what they did and how it helped you pick up the work.

A small improvement that would help

An ordinary handover omits a detail you need. Check the facts, explain the effect and make a reasonable request.

A reason to hold back

You notice something that may only be your preference, or you don't yet have the facts. Choose to leave it, clarify or wait rather than forcing feedback.

When an opportunity appears

1NoticeWould feedback genuinely help? What actually happened?
2Make a clear pointUse Situation, Behaviour and Impact where helpful. Add a request, suggestion or question only when appropriate.
3Learn from itDid the person understand? Did you learn something? What would you keep or change next time?

It may take a few attempts before this starts to feel natural. After your first genuine opportunity, use Stage 6 for a fuller review. Then continue practising in Stage 7. You are not finished after one attempt.

What about receiving feedback?

Stay alert when a colleague or manager offers feedback, whether or not you asked for it. Hear the point, clarify anything unclear and consider what it warrants. If nobody offers feedback, you can still practise giving it; you don't need to wait.

If it would be useful to understand how your work affects someone else, you can ask a specific person a focused question. Seeking feedback is an option, not an additional practice requirement.

Preparing for a planned feedback opportunity

If you're giving: What do you actually know? Why might it help? What is the smallest clear point worth making? Is this a suitable time and route? Use the or if helpful.

If feedback is arriving now: Hear the whole point. Ask for an example if unclear. Notice your initial reaction without treating it as the verdict; you can decide later what to do. If seeking deliberately, choose a specific question and someone who can genuinely answer.

You can prepare briefly if the opportunity is foreseeable, or use these questions afterwards if it arose unexpectedly. You do not need to fill in a preparation form.

Optional technique: arranging an observation in advance

Read this guidance before requesting an advance observation. An agreed observation can help when you want information about an upcoming task that you could not easily obtain otherwise.

Choose a willing colleague who can observe the relevant work. Agree one or two observable aspects, what they can realistically notice and when you will talk afterwards. Respect workload, confidentiality, customer and workplace restrictions; do not ask someone to assess your character or watch work they cannot properly observe.

For example, before a project review, you might ask a colleague who is already attending: “Could you notice whether I start speaking again before people have had a chance to answer my questions? Could we talk for five minutes afterwards?”

Afterwards, they say you generally allowed time, but twice started suggesting your own answer almost immediately. Ask for a specific example. They recall the delivery-risk discussion, when they were about to mention a supplier issue but you started listing risks yourself. Ask what effect they experienced, when people did have time and whether anything else stood out.

The agreed focus may influence what they notice. Their account is partial: separate observations from conclusions, consider contrary examples and use the receiving-feedback approach to decide what the information warrants.

Now move into normal work

Notice opportunities as work happens. Begin with something useful and manageable. If no suitable opportunity arises immediately, keep noticing; don't invent one. Return after your first attempt, even if it wasn't perfect.

Stage 6 · Learn from an early attempt

Learn from your first attempt

Use one experience to make the next attempt easier. You will continue practising after this review.

First review, not the end of practice

Think about one genuine situation in which you gave feedback, considered giving it, received it or sought it. What did you do? What happened? What would you keep or adjust for the next opportunity?

If you haven't had a suitable opportunity, return to . Don't invent an experience to complete this review.

Use the receiving or giving prompts that fit what happened; use both only if both were relevant. Think them through, use the notes space if it helps, then record only the two conclusions worth keeping below.

If no suitable situation has arisen, return to Stage 5 and allow more time. Do not invent an experience to answer these questions.

If a situation arose but you did not use the approach, think about what happened, what got in the way and what could help you recognise or handle a similar moment next time. Use the concluding notes below to retain what you learned.

More help: judging the usefulness of an experience

Judging the usefulness of an experience

Use these considerations alongside the reflection questions below. They are not a separate recording activity, and not every exchange needs to produce every outcome.

What to considerWhat useful evidence might look like
Your behaviourYou judged the opportunity appropriately, made the point clear or clarified feedback before judging it.
Information exchangedA relevant observation, missing detail or different perspective became available.
Immediate responseThe person understood the point, even if they disagreed or needed time.
Subsequent decisionThere was a sound reason to reinforce, adjust, test, clarify, investigate, take no action or use another route.
Effect on the workLater experience suggests an adjustment helped or revealed what still needs attention.

Separate what you did from what happened and what you can reasonably conclude. Someone’s refusal to engage does not automatically mean your approach was poor. A pleasant exchange or immediate agreement does not establish usefulness. Where you have not yet tried an adjustment or cannot observe its effects, leave that conclusion open.

If you received feedback

What did the feedback help you understand? What appeared to be direct observation and what was interpretation? Was anything vague or missing important context? Did the person have a perspective that was useful for this issue? What, if anything, will you continue, adjust, test, investigate further or leave unchanged?

If you deliberately sought the feedback, also ask whether you chose the right person and made the question specific enough to produce useful information.

If you gave - or considered giving - feedback

If you gave feedback, did the point land? “Landed” does not mean agreement; it means the other person understood what you were raising and why it mattered. Did their response add information or change your understanding? Looking back, was giving the feedback the right judgement?

If you decided not to give feedback, what led to that decision? Was it good judgement, or might you have been avoiding a useful conversation? Is anything still unresolved? What would make the situation worth revisiting - stronger evidence, a repeated pattern, better timing or another route?

Stage 7 · Ongoing workplace practice

Keep using feedback in normal work

You're entering a period of repeated practice. Look for useful, manageable opportunities to give feedback; notice and handle feedback you receive whenever it arises.

The practice loop
Notice→Give useful feedback→Review briefly→Try again

You can repeat this loop as opportunities arise. You do not need to record every instance, complete a quota or deliberately create feedback situations.

Start where the work makes it easy

Notice useful contributions worth reinforcing: a helpful handover, a timely warning, a clear explanation or something that made collaboration easier. Make the behaviour and impact specific. As the approach becomes more natural, use it for straightforward improvements too.

Receiving feedback is part of the capability, but you don't have to rely on others giving it to you. When it arrives, practise listening, clarifying and deciding what it warrants. You may also seek a focused perspective if it would help your work.

Guidance for keeping practice realistic

Do not turn practice into a feedback quota

Keep your personal development focus from Stage 3 in view. You do not need equal amounts of giving and receiving. Use genuine opportunities to practise the aspect you want to improve, while learning from other useful exchanges too. Sometimes the most skilful action is to seek a perspective, give feedback, understand feedback that arrives without warning, or decide not to use feedback.

Repeatedly asking the same person for feedback can also become burdensome unless you have both agreed that they are supporting your development. Use feedback because it is useful, not because you need another practice example.

Repeat before you stretch

After one successful attempt, the next useful step may simply be to handle a similar situation more consistently. Harder is not automatically better. Build a reliable base before deliberately choosing situations with more ambiguity, disagreement, sensitivity or consequence.

A · Written explanation

Some information is difficult to get on your own

You can see some of the results of your own work for yourself. But other people often have information that you cannot easily generate alone. A colleague may experience your handover, use your work, work alongside you during a busy period, hear how you explained something, or see an effect that is less obvious from your position.

Their perspective can help you adjust something, repeat something that works, investigate a problem earlier or understand how your work and behaviour affect other people. In the same way, you may hold useful information about how somebody else’s work or behaviour affects you and the work you share.

Feedback is not only about correcting mistakes

Everyday workplace feedback can concern the work itself, the way people work together, or something worth reinforcing because it helped.

Work or performance feedback might concern an output, a handover, a decision, clarity, timing, coordination or what made a task easier or harder to complete.

Behaviour feedback might concern how someone communicates, collaborates, interrupts, responds under pressure or affects people working around them.

Reinforcing feedback identifies something that worked and why it helped, making the useful information more specific than a general “thanks” or “good job”.

These often overlap. A behaviour matters because it affects the work, the relationship or the working environment.

Why feedback? Someone else may see an effect of your work that you cannot see yourself — and you may see an effect of theirs.

C · Explanation through an example

Other people can see and experience things about our work that we cannot always observe ourselves. Feedback can make that information available. It may concern a result, someone's behaviour or something that worked well.

Consider a handover

Someone receives a document from a colleague. It contains the necessary information, but the key decision is difficult to find.

What could useful feedback add to this situation?

“The information is there, but I struggled to find the decision I needed. Putting it at the beginning would help me act on the handover faster.”

The colleague who prepared the document may not have experienced that difficulty. This gives them information about the work's effect, rather than just a vague judgement.

What about something that worked?

Another handover placed the decision and next steps on its first page.

“Having the decision and next steps up front made it easy to pick this up quickly. Please keep that layout for future handovers.”

This reinforces a useful approach by identifying what helped and why, rather than offering only general praise.

Important distinction

Work feedback, behaviour feedback and reinforcing feedback can overlap. The purpose here is to recognise what information might be useful, not to score a correct classification or practise a complete feedback conversation.
