Make Feedback Useful

A development guide for using feedback more effectively with colleagues

Feedback can help you see things about your work that are difficult to see for yourself. It can also help colleagues understand how their work or behaviour affects you. The difficult part is not knowing that feedback exists. It is using it well in real working relationships.

This guide helps you practise two connected capabilities: giving useful feedback when it is worth giving, and receiving feedback well enough to decide what it means. Sometimes feedback arrives without you asking for it. Sometimes you deliberately seek another person’s perspective because they can see something you cannot.

How to use this guide

This is not designed to be completed in one sitting. Work through the early sections in short sittings if that suits your work. They help you understand the capability and prepare for practice. You then make manageable attempts, notice what happens, adjust your approach and gradually use the capability in a wider range of normal work situations.

What the journey involves

Get started — Understand why feedback matters, recognise where it shows up in your work, choose something you want to handle better and examine your current approach.

Learn the approach — Explore how to judge when feedback would help, make your message clear, understand feedback you receive and seek a useful perspective when needed.

Practise and adjust — Prepare for manageable feedback exchanges in relatively safe conditions. Try the approach more than once, notice what happens and make useful adjustments before increasing the challenge.

Use it in normal work — Apply the approach when genuine situations arise. Build consistency, use your judgement about when feedback is worthwhile and extend to more demanding situations when you are ready.

Review and consolidate — Look across your experiences, identify what has changed and what still needs attention, and decide what you will continue or develop next.

About the journey and time commitment

This is a spaced workplace-development journey, not a short course. Allow roughly 30–45 minutes to get started and 20–30 minutes to learn the approach. You can spread this time across shorter sittings.

Practice involves preparing a message or response, having a feedback exchange and learning from it. Preparation may take only a few minutes; the exchange takes however long is appropriate to the situation. You do not need a formal review or a written account after every attempt. Return while enough detail remains to recall what you tried and what happened, and retain a brief useful observation where appropriate. After several attempts, set aside time to consider the pattern, what is improving and what to try next.

Later, allow roughly 30–45 minutes for a more deliberate review and consolidation. These are estimates of time spent using the guide, not targets for the length or number of feedback conversations. The overall journey depends on the opportunities available and your development needs. Do not create unnecessary feedback situations simply to finish the guide.

Thinking, writing and support

You do not need to write down every thought or fill every notes area. Think through the prompts, then record the short conclusions worth returning to. A few words may be enough. The time estimates allow for thinking and review; they are not required sitting lengths.

Do not create feedback opportunities simply to complete an exercise. Use real situations where feedback could genuinely help.

You can complete the guide privately and independently. If a manager, colleague, peer, mentor or another trusted person can usefully support your development, involve them where that adds value. They are a sounding board, not an assessor.

The Feedback Job Aid is at the end. It is signposted again when you start applying the capability so you can find it quickly.

The aim

The aim is not more feedback. It is better judgement about when feedback is useful, clearer information when it is exchanged, and better decisions about what follows.

Stage 6

Use it in a real feedback situation

Choose a genuine, manageable feedback opportunity connected to your Stage 2 focus, then use the relevant route below.

Practise the aspect you chose: giving, receiving or deliberately seeking feedback. You do not need to complete the preparation for both giving and receiving.

Do not manufacture an opportunity or choose your most sensitive situation simply to prove that you can do it. If another useful feedback situation arises first, you can learn from it too. Keep your chosen focus in view and allow more time for a genuine opportunity to practise it.

Two speeds, one capability

If you can see the situation coming, take a few minutes to prepare using the relevant prompts below.

If it is already happening, use the simplest useful checks in the moment. You can do the fuller reflection afterwards in Stage 7.

If you are receiving feedback

The feedback may arrive without you asking, or you may deliberately seek another perspective. Either way, your first job is to understand the point before deciding what it means.

If it is happening now

Hear the whole point.

If something is unclear, ask what the person means or ask for an example.

Notice your first reaction without treating it as the verdict.

You can decide what to do with the feedback later.

If you are deliberately seeking feedback and have time to prepare, decide what specific perspective you want and who can genuinely provide it. Use the space below if it helps. You only need enough preparation to make the request useful.

Optional notes — use this space if writing helps you think. You do not need to fill it.

If you are thinking about giving feedback

If it is happening now

What do I actually know?

Does this need saying now?

If yes, make the smallest clear point that is useful. If no, get more information, wait, let it go or use another route.

If you can prepare, slow the judgement down once. Think about what happened, what you may be assuming, why it matters, whether you have enough information and whether this is the right time and route.

Optional notes — use this space if writing helps you think. You do not need to fill it.

If you decide not to give feedback, or decide that you first need more information or another route, that is still a real application of the capability. Stage 7 will help you examine whether the judgement was sound or whether avoidance may have played a part.

Now put the guide down

Use the relevant approach in a genuine, manageable situation connected to your chosen focus. Return to Stage 7 afterwards, whether you gave feedback, received or sought it, or considered giving it and decided another response was more useful.

If you only used part of the approach or recognised the moment afterwards, bring that experience too. You do not need a successful outcome before returning.

Stage 7

What did the experience teach you?

Review your first application and decide what to remember and what to try or notice next time.

Use the receiving or giving prompts that fit what happened; use both only if both were relevant. Think them through, use the notes space if it helps, then record only the two conclusions worth keeping below.

If no suitable situation has arisen, return to Stage 6 and allow more time. Do not invent an experience to answer these questions.

If a situation arose but you did not use the approach, think about what happened, what got in the way and what could help you recognise or handle a similar moment next time. Use the concluding notes below to retain what you learned.

If you received feedback

What did the feedback help you understand? What appeared to be direct observation and what was interpretation? Was anything vague or missing important context? Did the person have a perspective that was useful for this issue? What, if anything, will you continue, adjust, test, investigate further or leave unchanged?

If you deliberately sought the feedback, also ask whether you chose the right person and made the question specific enough to produce useful information.

If you gave — or considered giving — feedback

If you gave feedback, did the point land? “Landed” does not mean agreement; it means the other person understood what you were raising and why it mattered. Did their response add information or change your understanding? Looking back, was giving the feedback the right judgement?

If you decided not to give feedback, what led to that decision? Was it good judgement, or might you have been avoiding a useful conversation? Is anything still unresolved? What would make the situation worth revisiting — stronger evidence, a repeated pattern, better timing or another route?

Use this space if writing helps you think. You do not need to fill it.

Optional: talk it through with someone

If you have a manager, colleague, peer, mentor or someone else you trust, this can be a useful point to talk the experience through.

They may help you see another perspective, test an assumption or think about what you want to try next. If they know what you are developing, they may also be able to notice change over time.

Use them as a sounding board, not as an assessor.

Feedback Job Aid

Keep this page easy to find. Use it before, during if practical, or shortly after a feedback situation.

If you are thinking about giving feedback

STEP BACK

What actually happened? What might I be assuming? Is this worth raising? Is this the right time and route?

MAKE THE POINT CLEAR

Describe what happened and the effect it had. Explain why it matters where useful. Be clear enough for the other person to understand the point.

RESPOND TO WHAT HAPPENS

They may take it in, add information or disagree. Listen where there is something useful to understand. Do not force a discussion.

If you are receiving feedback

LISTEN AND CLARIFY

Hear the whole point. If something is unclear, ask what the person means or ask for an example. You do not need to agree immediately.

THINK ABOUT THE INFORMATION

What appears to be observation and what is interpretation? Does this person have a useful perspective? Does other evidence support or challenge it? Do you need time before deciding?

DECIDE

Continue something? Adjust something? Test a change? Find out more? Take no action?

If you need another perspective

SEEK IT DELIBERATELY

What specific area do you want to understand, and who has actually seen, used or experienced it? Ask a focused question that is easy to answer.

After a feedback situation that mattered

Quick review

What happened?

What did I learn?

What will I keep or change next time?
